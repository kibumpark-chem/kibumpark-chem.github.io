About

I work at the intersection of chemistry, physics, and computation to understand how atoms and molecules interact. In particular, my research has focused on how proteins navigate their energy landscapes as they fold into their native structures—a process that is not always guaranteed to succeed. I am interested in the physical principles underlying protein folding and in the factors that determine the reliability of molecular simulations, including model accuracy, conformational sampling, and uncertainty quantification.

My work centers on molecular simulation and machine learning to connect molecular mechanisms with the larger length scales and timescales relevant to biology. I use experimental data to inform and validate these approaches, while developing methods and software to improve their accuracy, efficiency, and reliability.

I view computation as part of an iterative feedback loop in which experiments, physical theory, molecular simulations, and machine learning continually inform and refine one another. This perspective motivates my interest in approaches that combine the interpretability and physical grounding of mechanistic models with the flexibility and scalability of modern data-driven methods.

Looking ahead, I aim to deepen our understanding of the physical principles governing protein structure, dynamics, and dysfunction, while contributing to practical advances in protein and small-molecule therapeutics. In particular, I am interested in building computational tools that are accurate, robust, and interpretable, and that can address genuinely challenging problems in protein biophysics and drug discovery.

Research

I work on both sides of the same problem: the physical principles that govern how proteins behave, and the computational tools needed to study them at a useful scale.

  • Protein Folding & Quality Control

    Harvard University

    I study how proteins navigate complex folding landscapes and how cells help them avoid or escape non-native states. This work is organized around two connected questions: how molecular chaperones reshape folding pathways, and how proteins begin folding while they are still being synthesized.

    Chaperone-assisted folding

    I compare chaperone-dependent and chaperone-independent homologs to find the physical features that separate them. Using molecular simulation, I investigate how non-native contacts, chain topology, and hydrophobic-core formation create kinetic traps and reshape the folding landscape.

    • Compared folding landscapes of chaperone-dependent and -independent homologs, including actin, eEF1A, and Rli1.
    • Identified topological traps involving non-native contacts and hydrophobic-core expansion that can stabilize misfolded states.
    • Studied how chaperone systems alter the energetic and kinetic accessibility of productive folding pathways.

    Co-translational folding

    Folding does not always wait for synthesis to finish. I study how folding begins during translation, and how the timing of chemical events such as disulfide-bond formation guides a nascent chain toward its native structure.

    • Simulated co-translational folding of the SARS-CoV-2 receptor-binding domain.
    • Investigated how the timing of disulfide-bond formation influences the folding pathway and final structure.
    • Studied the role of ribosome-associated chaperones in vectorial folding of nascent proteins.

  • Conformational Sampling & Energy Models

    Method & software development

    Studying a folding landscape reliably takes two things: an energy function that ranks structures correctly, and sampling that can actually reach them. I work on both, and on the software that makes them usable.

    Knowledge-based force fields

    I am developing knowledge-based force fields that use experimental data to improve the description of protein energetics. Many current approaches rely on evolutionary information from multiple sequence alignments, which is powerful but less effective for individual point mutations that are poorly represented in the available data. My work instead incorporates experimentally measured information directly into the energy model.

    • Developing experimentally informed approaches for improving knowledge-based protein force fields.
    • Studying how force fields can better represent the energetic effects of point mutations.
    • Curating benchmark decoy sets that test whether a model can distinguish native and near-native structures from non-native decoys.

    Monte Carlo simulation

    I am developing pyMCPU, a Monte Carlo suite for protein simulation, built with protein folding, misfolding, and conformational landscapes in mind. Its Python and C++ components support efficient sampling, rapid prototyping, and the development of new simulation methods.

    • Building a Python and C++ framework for Monte Carlo simulation of proteins.
    • Developing cache-efficient data structures for fast sampling of protein energy landscapes.
    • Supporting rapid testing of new collective variables and sampling methods.
