About

When I was young, I believed that we could simulate everything in silico and obtain precise answers through computation. That belief led me to computational chemistry. As I gained experience, however, I became increasingly interested in the limitations of simulation: how accurately we describe the physical system, how efficiently we explore its conformational space, and how much confidence we can place in the resulting predictions.

I now study protein folding and biophysics at the intersection of chemistry, physics, and computation. My work focuses on how proteins fold, misfold, and maintain their structural integrity, as well as on the physical principles that shape these processes. I combine molecular simulation, experimentally informed modeling, and software development to connect molecular-scale detail with biologically relevant timescales.

Collaboration and tool building have shaped how I approach research. I see computation as part of a larger feedback loop: experiments, physical models, and simulation methods continually inform and refine one another. More accurate force fields, biophysical priors, and improved sampling methods can work together to preserve microscopic detail while making larger-scale questions computationally accessible.

Ultimately, I hope to develop a better understanding of protein biophysics and chemistry while contributing to practical applications in protein and small-molecule therapeutics. I am particularly interested in building reliable computational tools that can be used to investigate challenging biological systems.

Research

My research combines protein biophysics, computational chemistry, and software development. I am interested in both the physical principles governing protein behavior and the computational tools needed to study them efficiently.

  • Protein Folding & Quality Control

    Harvard University

    I study how proteins navigate complex folding landscapes and how cells help them avoid or escape non-native states. This work is organized around two connected questions: how molecular chaperones reshape folding pathways, and how proteins begin folding while they are still being synthesized.

    Chaperone-assisted folding

    I compare chaperone-dependent and chaperone-independent protein homologs to identify the physical features that distinguish proteins capable of folding independently from those that require assistance. Using molecular simulation, I investigate how non-native contacts, topology, and hydrophobic-core formation create kinetic traps and reshape the folding landscape.

    • Compared folding landscapes of chaperone-dependent and independent homologs, including actin, eEF1A, and Rli1.
    • Identified topological traps involving non-native contacts and hydrophobic-core expansion that can stabilize misfolded states.
    • Studied how chaperone systems alter the energetic and kinetic accessibility of productive folding pathways.

    Co-translational folding

    Proteins do not always fold after synthesis is complete. I study how folding begins during translation and how the timing of chemical events, including disulfide-bond formation, guides a nascent protein toward its native structure.

    • Simulated co-translational folding of the SARS-CoV-2 receptor binding domain.
    • Investigated how the timing of disulfide-bond formation influences the folding pathway and final structure.
    • Studied the role of ribosome-associated chaperones in vectorial folding of nascent proteins.

  • Knowledge-Based Force Fields

    Ongoing

    I am developing knowledge-based force fields that use experimental data to improve the description of protein energetics. The goal is to build models that better capture how individual sequence changes alter protein stability, structure, and function.

    Many current approaches rely heavily on evolutionary information derived from multiple-sequence alignments. While these methods are powerful, they can be less effective for understanding the effects of individual point mutations, particularly when a mutation is poorly represented or absent in available evolutionary data. My work instead focuses on incorporating experimentally measured information into the energy model.

    • Developing experimentally informed approaches for improving knowledge-based protein force fields.
    • Studying how force fields can better represent the energetic effects of point mutations.
    • Curating benchmark decoy sets for evaluating whether models can distinguish native-like structures from misfolded alternatives.

  • Monte Carlo Protein Simulation

    Software development

    I am developing pyMCPU, a general-purpose Monte Carlo suite for protein simulation, specialized for studying protein folding, misfolding, and conformational landscapes.

    The framework combines Python and C++ components to support efficient sampling, rapid prototyping, and the development of new simulation methods. The broader goal is to make advanced protein Monte Carlo simulations easier to extend, benchmark, and apply to biophysical questions.

    • Building a Python and C++ framework for Monte Carlo simulation of proteins.
    • Developing cache-efficient data structures for fast sampling of protein energy landscapes.
    • Supporting rapid testing of new collective variables and sampling methods.
    • Designing the software as a general-purpose protein Monte Carlo suite with particular emphasis on folding studies.
