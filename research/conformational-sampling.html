Conformational Sampling & Energy Models

Studying a folding landscape reliably takes two things: an energy function that ranks structures correctly, and sampling that reaches them efficiently without biasing their equilibrium populations. To make both practical, I have built pyMCPU, a complete Monte Carlo package for protein simulation that is now ready for release through pip and conda. On this foundation, I am developing statistical potentials for protein energetics and Monte Carlo moves that use knowledge-based and machine-learned priors while satisfying detailed balance.

pyMCPU

pyMCPU is a Monte Carlo package for protein simulation, built with folding, misfolding, and conformational landscapes in mind. It is complete and ready for public release through pip and conda, so it can be installed and used like any other scientific Python package.

The package pairs a C++ engine, which uses cache-efficient data structures for fast sampling, with a Python interface for rapid prototyping. New collective variables and sampling methods can be implemented and tested quickly, which makes pyMCPU the platform for the methods described below.

Statistical potentials

I develop statistical potentials: energy functions derived from the statistics of experimentally determined protein structures. Because such a potential is defined over three-dimensional structure, the same function that ranks conformations can also drive the simulations that explore them.

Many current approaches to protein modeling draw on evolutionary information from multiple sequence alignments. That information is powerful, but it is less effective for individual point mutations that are poorly represented in the available data. My work instead incorporates experimental measurements directly into the potential, with the aim of representing the energetic effects of point mutations more accurately.

To evaluate these potentials, I curate benchmark decoy sets that test whether a model can distinguish native and near-native structures from non-native decoys. This is a basic requirement for simulation: a potential that cannot rank native structures above their alternatives cannot be trusted to describe a folding landscape.

Sampling under detailed balance

Monte Carlo simulation explores a landscape by proposing moves and accepting or rejecting them. When the moves satisfy detailed balance, the simulation samples the equilibrium distribution defined by the energy function, so the populations and free energies it reports reflect the potential rather than artifacts of the move set. My aim is to make moves more efficient without giving up this guarantee.

Knowledge-based moves

In a densely packed protein, random perturbations are frequently rejected, which slows sampling. Knowledge-based moves instead propose local conformations drawn from the statistics of known protein structures, making each proposal more likely to be accepted. Because these proposals are biased, the acceptance step corrects for that bias, as detailed balance requires. The moves therefore speed up sampling without shifting the equilibrium.

Machine-learned priors

I am extending this approach with data-driven priors: proposal distributions learned from structural data using machine learning. Learned models can represent correlated, high-dimensional conformational distributions that are difficult to capture with tabulated statistics. Because the same detailed-balance correction applies, these priors can accelerate exploration of a landscape while leaving its equilibrium unchanged.

Outlook

My long-term goal is to make simulations of protein conformational landscapes both reliable and practical at scale. That means pairing interpretable statistical potentials with machine-learned components that accelerate sampling, while preserving the thermodynamic guarantees on which trustworthy simulation depends.

Releasing pyMCPU through pip and conda is a step toward making these methods available to other researchers. Ultimately, I hope these tools will help address questions from my protein folding research, such as how non-native interactions give rise to alternative conformations and kinetic traps, across many more proteins.
