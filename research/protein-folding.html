Protein Biophysics & Computational Biology

Protein Folding

Protein sequence shapes an energetic landscape containing native states, kinetic barriers, and competing non-native conformations. My research focuses on characterizing these landscapes computationally and connecting their physical features to biological function. I also investigate how protein biogenesis—including translation, the ribosome, and molecular chaperones—perturbs these landscapes and redirects folding in cellular contexts.

Conformational landscapes and phenotype

My primary research focuses on characterizing the conformational landscapes populated by proteins and connecting these states to biological function. Using molecular simulation, computational modeling, and high-throughput structural data, I investigate how non-native interactions, chain topology, and energetic frustration give rise to alternative conformations and kinetic traps.

A central goal is to move beyond a binary distinction between folded and misfolded states. Distinct conformational ensembles can have different structures, dynamics, stabilities, and biological consequences. By mapping these states and relating them to phenotype, I seek to identify physical principles that connect protein sequence to function.

In one line of work, I contributed simulations to a high-throughput disulfide-scanning study of the disordered chaperone HdeA; collaborators developed and ran the scanning experiments. By simulating disulfide-trapped HdeA variants, I proposed structural features (hydrophobicity and dimer dissociation) that we then compared with in vivo toxicity.

Folding in cellular context

I extend this landscape-based perspective to the biological environments in which folding occurs. In collaborative studies, I have investigated how molecular chaperones, the ribosome, translation, and cotranslational chemical events alter the conformational routes available to nascent proteins.

Chaperone-modulated folding

Chaperones can recognize vulnerable intermediates, stabilize non-native states, and redirect proteins toward productive folding pathways. Yet homologs of the same protein can differ in whether they need a chaperone at all. I compare the simulated folding landscapes of homologs that do and do not depend on a chaperone, alongside experiments by the Denic, Frydman, and Roh labs. I use these comparative folding landscapes to pinpoint the features, such as hydrophobicity, that set the two groups apart.

Across the actin, eEF1A, and Rli1/ABCE1 homologs, the causes of chaperone dependence differ. In actin, dependence traces to disrupted packing of the compact subdomain 1 (SD1) core; in eEF1A and Rli1, non-native contacts stabilize non-native intermediates. These systems illustrate how cellular folding machinery can modulate, rather than simply accelerate, the exploration of a sequence-dependent landscape.

For Rli1, I studied Ctr86 as its dedicated chaperone and needed structures of the yeast Rli1–Ctr86 complex to generate poses, but AlphaFold3 confidently predicted only the human ABCE1–Ataxin-10 complex. I therefore built OrthoPairMSA, which assembles species-paired multiple sequence alignments from ortholog groups (eggNOG, OrthoDB, or InterPro), pairing one representative per shared species by NCBI taxonomy ID, and writes inputs for AlphaFold3, Protenix, and Boltz. With these alignments, the interface confidence score (ipTM) for Rli1–Ctr86 rose from 0.67 to 0.80, which made pose generation possible.

Cotranslational folding

Folding begins before protein synthesis is complete. In collaborative studies, we investigated how the vectorial emergence of a nascent chain, its interaction with the ribosome, and the timing of chemical events such as disulfide-bond formation reshape the folding landscape during synthesis.

For the SARS-CoV-2 receptor-binding domain (RBD), we examined how cotranslational disulfide formation can favor native folding. My simulations of RBD synthesis predicted that native cysteine pairs come together partway through translation, before the protein can misfold into an aggregation-prone state. In a separate study of eEF1A, we investigated how a ribosome-associated chaperone and GTP binding cooperate to protect a vulnerable nascent domain and promote productive folding.

Outlook

My long-term goal is to develop computational and biophysical frameworks that connect sequence, conformational landscapes, and biological phenotype. Folding constraints represent one component of protein evolution alongside biochemical function, stability, dynamics, aggregation, and cellular context.

Incorporating folding energetics, kinetics, and conformational heterogeneity into sequence-based analysis may provide a complementary way to interpret functional variation in large metagenomic datasets. Such an approach could help distinguish sequence changes that alter biochemical activity from those that primarily affect folding behavior or dependence on cellular assistance.
